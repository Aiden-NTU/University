# How the design addresses the SDGs

In 2015 every member state of the United Nations adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable
Development, built around 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 169 more specific targets
[@un2015]. The goals cover poverty, health, education, energy, cities and the climate, and they
are meant to be tackled together. A building cannot meet a global goal on its own, but it can
contribute to particular targets, and that is how we have mapped the design.

## Goal 7: Affordable and clean energy

This is the goal the design addresses most directly. The roof generates about {{pv_yield_mwh}}
MWh of renewable electricity a year (target 7.2). Efficiency (target 7.3) comes from reusing
heat that a conventional building would throw away, and from lighting that needs
{{lighting_saving_pct}}% less electricity than a standard fit-out.

## Goal 9: Industry, innovation and infrastructure

Treating a small data centre as a building's heating plant is an unusual combination and is the
main innovation in the design. It turns two separate pieces of infrastructure, computing and
heating, into one more efficient system (target 9.4).

## Goal 12: Responsible consumption and production

Materials are chosen to be renewable, reused or recycled wherever possible. Harvested rainwater
replaces drinking-quality mains water for flushing, and the refill station reduces single-use
plastic bottles (targets 12.2 and 12.5).

## Goal 13: Climate action

The timber structure stores carbon instead of emitting it, and the building's low energy use
reduces emissions every year it is in use. Target 13.3 is about education and awareness, and the
control room makes the building's energy use visible to everyone who studies in it.

## Goals 4 and 11

The Heat Exchange is a place of learning, and the building itself becomes a teaching resource
for sustainable design (target 4.7). Replacing a car park with green space and cycle facilities
supports sustainable transport and accessible public space (targets 11.2 and 11.7).
