# Sustainable technologies

## On-site renewable generation

The sawtooth roof carries **114 photovoltaic modules**, **49 kWp** in total, on south-facing
slopes at **25°**. For Clifton’s latitude, PVGIS (PVGIS-SARAH3 climate data) estimates about
**46,924 kWh/yr**, or roughly **958 kWh per kWp** [@pvgis]. At 24.5 p/kWh [@desnz_qep2026] that
generation is worth about **£11,500/yr** if used on site. An indicative installed cost of about
£1,000 per kWp gives a one-off outlay of roughly **£49,000** for the roof array (excluding
scaffolding and structural fixings), so simple payback against avoided electricity is on the order
of four to five years before maintenance—indicative only, pending a contractor quote.

Grid connection of generation plant at this scale must follow the Distribution Network Operator
process in Engineering Recommendation G99 [@g99]. The university would also need to clear the
array through its estates and planning processes as a non-domestic rooftop installation. The
sawtooth form is chosen so that PV sits on the sun-facing slope while the opposite face provides
north light to the first floor—combining generation with daylight in one roof geometry.

## Heat recovery from computing

Most buildings pay to cool computers and then pay again to heat rooms. Here a small **edge server
room** (four racks at an assumed 3 kW IT load each) produces about **105 MWh/yr** of heat that
would otherwise be rejected. Rear-door heat exchangers and a coolant loop feed a **water-source
heat pump** in the plant room, raising the lukewarm water to about 45–55 °C for low-temperature
heating, with a thermal store to buffer supply and demand. European energy-efficiency law
increasingly expects waste heat from computing to be reused where feasible [@eu2023_1791]. The
same idea is applied here at building scale. Heating demand still needs a full energy model
before the heat pump can be sized finally; until then plant costs are marked “quote needed” in
the cost schedule.

## Lighting and controls

All teaching and study spaces use **LED panels** with **occupancy and daylight sensors**, so lights
dim or switch off when rooms are empty or bright enough—matching common NTU campus practice.
Against a fluorescent, always-on baseline, the design schedule uses about **4,021 kWh/yr** for
lighting instead of about **18,923 kWh/yr**, a saving of roughly **79%** (about £3,650/yr at
24.5 p/kWh) [@desnz_qep2026]. Wiring would follow standard low-voltage distribution with
centralised lighting control (for example DALI-style dimming). Accessible door operators and
sensor lighting are treated as part of everyday inclusive design, not as optional extras
[@adm2015].
